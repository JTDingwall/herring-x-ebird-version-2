Resource pulses concentrate food in space and time. Mobile consumers able to locate them gain access to energy that is unavailable for most of the year (Yang et al. 2008; Armstrong et al. 2016). Pacific herring (Clupea pallasii) spawning is among the largest of these pulses on the British Columbia coast. That delivery has been narrowing. The Strait of Georgia spawning range contracted through the twentieth century, abandoning historical sites in favour of a single dominant region on the northwest side of the Strait (Ware and Tovey 2004; Dingwall et al. 2026), and the spawning season has shortened at both ends over the same period [[CITATION]]. A contracted pulse is a shorter feeding season reached over a longer distance; a consumer that extends its opportunity by tracking successive events has fewer events to track and further to travel between them (Armstrong et al. 2016; Lok et al. 2012). How the coastal bird community responds to that pulse is undescribed at the scale on which ecosystem-based management of a forage fish now operates (Ethier et al. 2020; de Zwaan et al. 2024).
Spawning concentrates adults into shallow water in late winter and early spring, where they deposit adhesive eggs on intertidal and shallow-subtidal vegetation (Haegele and Schweigert 1985; Dingwall et al. 2026). At a single shoreline the event can pass within days; the eggs then persist for roughly two weeks before hatching or washing away. Food therefore becomes available in a sequence. Adult fish are accessible while they hold in the shallows, attached eggs remain after the adults leave, and dislodged eggs and spent carcasses accumulate at the surface and along the strandline. Birds reach each of these by different means, and can remove a substantial fraction of a season's egg deposition (Haegele 1993; Bishop and Green 2001).
Direct evidence that birds respond to herring spawn comes from a small number of focused studies. Harlequin Ducks (Histrionicus histrionicus) aggregate at spawning sites in the Strait of Georgia (Rodway et al. 2003); Surf Scoters (Melanitta perspicillata) and White-winged Scoters (M. deglandi) move beyond their ordinary winter ranges toward spawn and track successive events through spring migration (Lewis, Esler, and Boyd 2007; Lok et al. 2008, 2012); shoreline counts document large numerical aggregations during spawn (T. M. Sullivan, Butler, and Boyd 2002); and lagged spawning biomass improves waterbird abundance forecasts in Tomales Bay (Kelly, Rothenbach, and Weathers 2018). These studies are strong on the species they follow. They are also selective by design. Each begins from a taxon already known to aggregate, at a small number of sites, over one or a few seasons. Such work cannot establish how many species in a coastal community carry an association with spawning across many events and many years, whether species that feed in different ways respond at different points within an event, or which plausible consumers show nothing. The last of these carries the most weight. A regional description in which every group responds is difficult to attribute to herring. One in which responses follow how a bird feeds, and in which birds that cannot reach a shoreline deposit stay flat, is far harder to explain any other way. The breadth and internal structure of the response therefore remain undescribed at the scale on which spawn is surveyed and managed.
Community science records offer a different kind of leverage. However, it is worth being specific about what kind. eBird contributors have recorded birds along this coast for two decades, a span covering more than a thousand surveyed spawning events; a field season covers one, or a few. That difference matters because the resource itself moves. The shoreline, extent and date of spawning shift among years, so a fixed study site captures the pulse in some years and misses it in others, whereas a record tied to wherever spawn was surveyed follows it. Taxonomic breadth matters as much as duration. Targeted work begins from species already believed to respond. Its conclusions are conditioned on that choice. A complete checklist records every species the observer identified, so birds with no expected herring association enter on the same footing as scoters and gulls. Absences become measurable rather than assumed. Two decades, an entire coastal community, and every surveyed event is a combination no field programme has been funded to produce.
These records are not censuses. A checklist reports what an observer detected and chose to write down, and both depend on effort, skill, season and weather (Bird et al. 2014; Johnston et al. 2021). Complete checklists and their effort information support repeated comparison once that variation is carried into the analysis (B. L. Sullivan et al. 2009; Kelling et al. 2019), and the properties that allow comparison across sites and years also allow it across the days surrounding a single event. Whether a food-web signal is resolvable at that scale, rather than at the seasonal and regional scales for which these data are normally used, is unknown.
This study links 1,120 recorded spawning events to 217,200 complete eBird checklists collected in the Strait of Georgia between 2005 and 2025. For each of 49 coastal bird species, checklists within 5 km of a recorded event were compared with checklists 5 to 20 km away, before and after the event date, so that seasonal change shared by both zones and standing differences between shorelines fall out of the comparison. Two parts of the record were treated separately: whether a species was reported at all, and how many individuals were counted when a number was supplied. Species were assigned to feeding groups before any model was fitted.
The two explanations predict different patterns. If birds track the spawn, responses should concentrate near events in space and time, should separate among feeding groups in the order that adult fish, attached eggs and stranded material become available, and should be absent in birds that cannot reach a shoreline deposit. If the pattern instead reflects where observers looked, responses should appear across conspicuous taxa regardless of feeding mode, should not separate in time, and should extend to terrestrial birds recorded on the same checklists.
